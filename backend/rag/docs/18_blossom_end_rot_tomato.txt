DOC_ID: 18_blossom_end_rot_tomato
TITLE: Blossom-end rot (BER) - a physiological disorder, not an infection
CROPS: tomato, also pepper, brinjal, squash
SENSOR_SIGNALS: soil_moisture_pct swinging between dry and wet; high temperature spells; high soil_ec_dsm (salinity); very bright sun after cloudy weather

FACTS
- BER is a physiological problem, not caused by fungi or bacteria, and does not spread from plant to plant [S1]. Fungicides and insecticides do not control it [S2].
- Symptom: tan, water-soaked area at the blossom end that turns black and leathery and can reach a third of the fruit [S3].
- Cause is commonly given as local calcium shortage in the fruit, driven by water stress. Ontario states it is primarily caused by dry soil conditions rather than lack of calcium in the soil, and some scientists doubt low fruit calcium is the trigger [S4]. Illinois notes the cause is poorly understood and two views exist [S5].
- Factors listed: dry or fluctuating soil moisture, overwatering, root damage, waterlogged or cold soil, high temperatures and intense sun after cooler overcast weather, high ammonium or high nitrogen, high salinity, high potassium or magnesium, susceptible varieties, stress during rapid fruit growth [S3][S4][S5][S6]. Root rots, nematodes and soil compaction can add to it [S2].
- In a drip-irrigation study, the salinity of irrigation water that clearly raised BER was 3-4 dS/m [S7].
- Rapid growth followed by sudden hot, dry conditions causes BER [S2].

MANAGEMENT (as stated by sources)
- Water deeply and on a regular schedule, especially in hot weather [S2]. Keep soil consistently moist; never let it dry out [S8].
- Foliar calcium sprays are not effective because calcium moves poorly to fruit [S5][S8].
- Soil test; plant in well-drained soil; fertilise moderately to avoid salt build-up and excessive growth; mulching may help; lime where soil calcium is low [S3]. Choose less susceptible varieties [S3][S5].
- Trellising and pruning can increase BER by adding stress [S3].
