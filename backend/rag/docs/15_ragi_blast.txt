TITLE: Finger millet (ragi) blast - Pyricularia/Magnaporthe grisea
CROPS: ragi / finger millet (Karnataka grows about 60% of India's finger millet area per ICAR 2025; about 40% per an older student source)
SENSOR_SIGNALS: humidity above 90%; mean temperature about 18-28 C (spore germination best 25-28 C); cloudy days (3+ in a row); frequent rain or drizzle; high nitrogen

FACTS
- Blast is the most important and destructive fungal disease of finger millet [S1][S2]. It causes leaf blast, neck blast and finger (ear) blast [S3].
- Favourable conditions listed in a TNAU-affiliated college course document: cloudy skies, frequent rain and drizzle, high nitrogen, humidity above 90%, and 25-28 C for spore germination [S2]. (Course slides, treat as secondary; UAS data in file 16 supports the humidity and cloud pattern.)
- Symptoms: grey spots on leaves that join and dry the leaf; neck blast blocks nutrients to the grain; finger blast starts at the tip of the finger and spreads to the base before turning brown; empty fingers form at seed set [S3]. Another ICAR/ISGPB paper adds neck blast shows browning then blackening near the ear with olive-grey growth, and brown chaffy fingers [S4].
- The disease occurs almost every year in finger millet [S5].
- Because ragi is mostly rainfed and grown by small farmers, chemical control is hard to apply widely; preventive measures are mostly followed [S5][S4].

MANAGEMENT (as stated by sources)
- Resistant varieties are always preferred; the ICAR table lists released blast-tolerant varieties, including UAS Bangalore's GPU series, for Karnataka [S3].
- Early sowing (July) reduced blast severity; crop rotations suggested: ragi-groundnut-maize, ragi-chickpea-wheat, ragi-cowpea/pigeonpea-sorghum [S2].
- One AICRP small-millets result: a chlorothalonil spray at ear-head formation was effective against neck and finger blast and grain mould [S6]. Follow label.
- Biocontrol: Pseudomonas fluorescens strains were maintained for use against blast and foot rot [S6].
